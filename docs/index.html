Welcome

Welcome to the learning line Quantitative Methods to Understand E&BI. Across three courses, we will learn how to turn questions about entrepreneurship, organizations, markets, and innovation into defensible quantitative evidence. This line will help you to learn not only how to understand research papers used in other courses and write a quantitative thesis, but also how to conduct rigorous data analysis at work.

Our aim is not simply to teach formulas or R commands. We want you to become a careful reader and producer of evidence: someone who can choose a method, explain its logic, interpret its output correctly, and recognize what the available data cannot establish.

By the end of the learning line, you should be able to:

  • translate an E&BI question into variables, comparisons, and testable claims;
  • work reproducibly with data in R and communicate what the output means;
  • conduct high-quality data analysis for work and research;
  • evaluate the strength and limits of evidence in research papers; and
  • design and complete a quantitative thesis.

How to use this book

The permanent menu on the left is organized into QM Basic, QM Intermediate, QM Advanced, and Supplementary resources. On a small (mobile) screen, open the menu with the button at the top of the page.

Within each course, follow the chapters in order:

  • Lecture chapters introduce you to a question and data and teach you how to conduct analysis in R and theory that allows to interpret and understand the output.
  • Tutorial chapters help you retrieve, combine, and apply what you have learned. Designed for guided practice and exam preparation.
  • Course exam resources include a cumulative formula sheet for QM Basic, a code-and-formula sheet for QM Intermediate, and a 100-point mock exam at the end of each of those courses.
  • Supplementary resources collect reusable help with R, mathematics, and external videos.

IMPORTANT: Each lecture includes required pre-class preparation, in-class work, and follow-up practice. Always check and cover the material that is marked as pre-class preparation in the lecture chapter of this textbook. Be consistent with the cycle of pre-class prep, in-class joint work, after-class practice.

Lecture preparation and external videos

“Before class” sections include a clearly marked “Video Preparation” box. Watch the listed required clips before the lecture and make brief notes on the indicated concepts. Basic primarily uses MIT OpenCourseWare; Intermediate uses Stanford’s Statistical Learning series. The External Video Guide separates required preparation, review when needed, and optional extension clips. Only the first category is compulsory. In-class theory sections also link directly to external explanations that you can replay after a lecture or an absence. External sources may use different notation or cover more material than this course; follow the notation and scope stated in this textbook.

R and main dataset

You will work with R throughout all three courses. You already met R in Programming for E&BI. If a command or workflow has become rusty, use the R Refresher and the earlier course as references.

Our shared dataset records pitches shown on the US television programme Shark Tank. In a televised pitch, entrepreneurs present a venture or product to a panel of investors known as sharks. They may ask for money in exchange for an ownership share; the investors question them, negotiate, and may announce an agreement during the episode.

Most chapters use a 19-variable teaching view derived from the Shark Tank US Dataset. One row represents one recorded televised pitch. The file contains 1,441 pitches from completed Seasons 1–16; incomplete Season 17 is excluded. Its variables include pitch and episode identifiers, venture industry and written description, the initial amount and equity requested, whether an agreement was announced on air, recorded deal terms, and the source’s broad presenter-gender-composition category.

This is an observational record of selected, edited television pitches—not a random sample of all entrepreneurs or ventures. An on-air agreement is not evidence that the investment was completed or that the venture later succeeded. The data can describe recorded pitches and associations among their variables, but they do not by themselves identify causal effects.

Tutorials and assessment

All three courses end with an exam with open-ended questions that account for 100% of the grade. In QM Basic, the written exam can ask you to verify, interpret, and describe supplied R output and solve probability and statistics tasks. In QM Intermediate and QM Advanced, you will work directly with data in R during an exam administered through TestVision. Tutorials in all courses are designed as exam preparation sessions.

Go directly to the QM Basic Formula Sheet, QM Basic Mock Exam, QM Intermediate Code Sheet, or QM Intermediate Mock Exam when preparing for assessment.

Working with AI

We provide a chatbot for this course: Tilly for QM Courseline. It is designed to support and guide you throughout the course. It can help with organizational questions (schedule, grade composition, etc) as well as act as an individual tutor when solving tasks together. You can also ask it to generate additional practice questions to prepare for the exam.

When you use AI, follow Attempt → Ask → Verify → Explain:

  1. Attempt to solve the question yourself and identify where you are stuck.
  2. Ask AI for support in the form of a hint, counterexample, or diagnostic question. Be precise and detailed when creating a prompt for AI (better prompt - better answer).
  3. Verify the response against the data, course notation, and R output. Verification becomes the key skill when working with AI.
  4. Explain the result in your own words to internalize the knowledge. Ask AI for feedback on this explanation and/or bring it as a question to lectures/tutorials and your classmates.
